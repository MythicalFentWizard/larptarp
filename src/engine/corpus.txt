The archive was never meant to be complete. It grew the way a river delta grows, by small deposits that nobody planned, and every new record changed the shape of the records around it. Clerks arrived and left. Each one added a drawer, a ledger, or a new way of numbering the old ledgers, and each one believed the system would finally settle once their own corrections were in place.

In the early years the collection was kept in a single room above the harbour office. The room held tide tables, shipping manifests, weather notes, and a long series of letters about the repair of the north pier. A visitor could read the whole collection in a week. By the third decade the room had become a building, and the building had become a district of storerooms connected by narrow corridors and handwritten signs.

The catalogue followed the collection, but always at a distance. A catalogue is a map of a territory that keeps moving, and the clerks who maintained it learned to describe each shelf as it had been rather than as it was. Entries pointed to other entries. Cross references pointed to drawers that had been renumbered. Some readers spent an entire afternoon following a chain of references only to arrive at the card where they had started.

This was not considered a failure. The senior clerks argued that a reader who follows a reference is already learning something about the structure of the archive, even when the reference leads nowhere. They kept a register of circular paths and studied it the way a surveyor studies the drift of a coastline. Each loop revealed a place where two departments had described the same object in different words.

The weather station on the eastern ridge contributed more paper than any other source. Every hour an observer recorded the pressure, the temperature, the direction of the wind, and the state of the sky. Every evening the observations were copied into a fair ledger and the rough notes were sent down the hill. The archive kept both versions. When the two disagreed, a note was added to explain the difference, and the note was given its own number in the catalogue.

Measurement has a habit of producing more measurement. Once the station began recording rainfall, the harbour office asked for the level of the river. Once the river was measured, the millers asked for the speed of the current at the weir. Each new series required a new form, each form required a new drawer, and each drawer required an entry in the index that described where the drawer could be found and which earlier series it continued.

The index itself became a subject of study. Students came to examine how the headings changed from one decade to the next. A heading that began as a simple place name might later be divided into parishes, then into streets, then into individual buildings, while older material remained filed under the original name. To find everything about a single house, a reader had to search under every name the house had ever carried.

Gardens appear throughout the collection in unexpected places. The account books of the harbour office record the purchase of seeds and fruit trees. The weather ledgers note the first frost and the date the almond trees flowered. A box of letters from a retired pilot describes, over many years, the slow construction of a walled garden behind his cottage, with careful lists of what grew well in salt air and what did not.

Some records exist only as copies of copies. A survey of the old salt marsh, for example, survives in a version traced from an earlier tracing, and each tracer added small corrections and small errors. Comparing the versions side by side shows the marsh changing shape across the generations of paper, although nobody can now say how much of that change happened in the marsh and how much happened in the hands of the copyists.

The relay office kept a log of every signal it passed along the coast. Most entries are routine. A ship has been sighted, a ship has arrived, the light at the point is working, the light at the point is not working. Read in sequence, however, the log becomes a kind of slow conversation between stations that never met, each one answering the last and waiting patiently for the next reply.

Over time the clerks developed a vocabulary for the different kinds of uncertainty they encountered. A record could be missing, misplaced, misdated, duplicated, superseded, or simply unreadable. Each condition had its own mark in the margin of the catalogue. A reader who learned the marks could tell at a glance whether a reference was worth following or whether it would lead into another corridor of revisions.

The archive still grows. New material arrives in boxes, and the boxes are opened, sorted, described, numbered, and shelved. Every description adds a few more links between the new material and the old. Somewhere in the building there is a drawer that holds the oldest surviving card in the catalogue, and somewhere else there is a card that refers to that drawer by a number it no longer uses.

Visitors are welcome, although they are advised to begin with the general guide and to keep notes of the paths they take. The guide explains the main series, the principal indexes, and the rules for requesting material from the closed stores. It also includes a short essay on patience, written by a former keeper, who observed that every archive rewards the reader who is willing to follow one more reference before deciding that the search is finished.
